# FRUS: what the calendar cites and what it does not

Nov. 26, 2026, about 10:15 p.m. PT. For the owner, on the question of 10:07 p.m.: "Have we ever done an inventory of uncited FRUS documents? It might make sense to conduct a volume-by-volume survey to see what we're missing, particularly in threads we've already read related literatures on (e.g., VN, the PRC, Cuba)."

## What we have done before

No such inventory has been made. But every FRUS document of the span is already in the series: the daybook lists each one under its day (from the volumes' own files, the microfiche supplements included), and the series holds the text of each (`sources/frus-text/`). So the inventory needs no reading. The table below is counted from those two sources and the calendar's links tonight.

## What it shows

Of 26,968 FRUS documents from January 1960 to January 1969, the calendar's entries cite 3,518, about one in eight. The volumes the calendar leans on most are the Vietnam volumes of 1964 to 1968 (a third to a half cited) and the Cuba volume of 1961 to 1962 (42 percent). The volumes on China, Laos, Cuba after 1962, and the microfiche supplements are hardly touched:

- **Vietnam:** 3,887 documents in eleven volumes (1961–63, I–IV; 1964–68, I–VII), 1,248 cited, 2,570 not.
- **China:** 1958–60, XIX, 3 of 49 cited; 1961–63, XXII (Northeast Asia: China, Korea and Japan), 64 of 383; 1964–68, XXX, 19 of 347.
- **Cuba:** 1958–60, VI, 56 of 213; 1961–63, X, 185 of 443; XI (the missile crisis), 75 of 390; the microfiche supplement to X–XII, 0 of 746; 1964–68, XXXII, 44 of 441.
- **Laos:** 1958–60, XVI, 24 of 226; 1961–63, XXIV, 53 of 491; 1964–68, XXVIII, 25 of 401.

An uncited document is not always a missing entry. Many record an event the calendar already has from a book; then the book is cited and the record is not, against R28 (the primary record first). Others record events the calendar lacks.

## A survey, volume by volume

1. **Sorting, by script (no reading).** For each uncited document, the script looks for calendar entries of the same day or the day before or after that name the same people (FRUS's lists of persons are already joined to the name entries) or fall in the volume's thread. It sorts the documents into three lists: those that match an entry (the entry lacks its record); those that match nothing (a likely missing entry); and the routine ones (transmittals, which R18 passes over). The result is a note for you with the counts for every volume, before any reading.
2. **Reading, by volume, Sonnet then Opus, in sequence.** For each document that matches an entry, the reader reads it and cites it first in the entry, correcting the entry against it where they differ (R28, R33b). For each that matches nothing, the reader reads it and enters the events it records (R5, R7), in the day's order (R25c). FRUS documents are short, about two pages each, so a reader takes about 75 documents. Vietnam's 2,570 would take about 35 readers; China's, Cuba's and Laos's together about 40 more; the whole series about 310.

## Where in the queue (the default taken, ruling 71)

- The sorting script now, beside the place pages: it reads nothing and costs little.
- The reading for Vietnam, China, Cuba and Laos, whose literature the calendar has read most, right after item 13a, the Soviet record. The other sides' records (China, the DRV, the Soviet Union) come first, then the American record against them.
- Every other FRUS volume with its subject's batch in the reading plan (the Middle East volumes with W6, Berlin with W3, and so on), each read before that batch's books.

## The sorting

Nov. 27, 2026. This is step 1 of the survey above. The script is `tools/daybook/frus_survey.py`; its rules are written at its head. It wrote one row for each uncited document to `notes/proposals/frus-survey.tsv`. It read no document beyond its heading and its date.

### What the script does

- It takes every FRUS document in the daybook and leaves out those a calendar entry links to. That leaves 23,422 documents. (The table above counts 23,450; 28 more have been cited since.)
- It puts each document in one of three groups. Group (a): it matches an entry. Group (b): it matches nothing, so it may be a missing entry. Group (c): it is routine, and R18 passes it over.
- Group (c) is found first. It holds the editorial notes and the covering notes and transmittals that say so in their headings ("transmitting", "covering", "enclosing", "with attached").
- The persons of a document are those who sent it, received it or took part in it, as its heading names them. The heading here is the title, the lists of persons under it (PARTICIPANTS, PRESENT, TO, FROM), and the author the daybook gives. The FRUS lists of persons, already joined to the series' name entries, tell who each one is.
- A document matches an entry by persons when the entry falls on the document's day, the day before or the day after, and tags one of the document's persons. The entry must also fall in the volume's thread, or tag two of the document's persons.
- A document whose heading names no person matches by thread: any entry of those days in the volume's thread.
- Everything else goes to group (b).
- Each volume's threads were chosen by hand from its title, against the names of the calendar's threads. Where a country has no thread of its own, the volume takes the thread its entries are filed under: Thailand's under Laos, Cambodia's under Vietnam, Burma's and Taiwan's under China. The map is in the script.

### Where the rules differ from the plan

- The plan matched an entry that tags any one of the document's persons. Tried that way, a document matched every entry of its days that tagged Rusk, McNamara or the President. So a match by one person now needs the volume's thread too, and a match outside the thread needs two persons.
- The President never counts alone. He is tagged on all his own acts, and most of them are on other matters.
- A telegram from the Department of State is not matched by its signer. The daybook gives the Secretary or the Acting Secretary as its author, because he signed it, not because he wrote it.
- A document matches by thread only when its heading names no person at all. A document whose persons are in no entry of its days goes to group (b), not to a thread match.
- The table has a column the plan did not name: "by", which says whether a match was by persons or by thread, or why a document is routine.

### How good the matching is

I checked 40 documents of group (a) and 40 of group (b), drawn at random, against their headings and the entries of their days. Where a heading gives a subject line, I read that too. A match is right when the entry records the very event the document records, so that the document is that entry's missing record. It is close when the entry is on the same matter but records another event: an earlier message, a reply, a meeting the document reports on.

Group (a), 40 documents (15 matched by persons, 25 by thread):

- Right: 4. Rusk's meeting on Laos of Mar. 12, 1961; the White House meeting of Jan. 18, 1964, at which the entry has Johnson doubt the sabotage raids on Cuba (the entry cites a history; the document is most likely its record); Rusk with Khrushchev at Pitsunda, Aug. 9, 1963; McNamara's draft memorandum on the 1964 Defense budget, Dec. 3, 1962.
- Close: 6. For example, Harriman and McCone by telephone the day the entry has McCone's summary of a CIA cable from Saigon; a telegram to Moscow in the arms control volume, during the test ban talks, the day of Rusk's message to Bonn on the ban.
- Wrong: 20. For example, a telegram to the embassy in Liberia matched to Lumumba in Brussels; a memorandum on an emissary to Cambodia matched to a telegram on the I Corps command.
- Cannot tell from the heading: 10. Most are bare telegrams on busy days ("Telegram From the Department of State to the Embassy in Vietnam").

By persons: 3 right, 3 close, 6 wrong, 3 cannot tell. By thread: 1 right, 3 close, 14 wrong, 7 cannot tell.

Group (b), 40 documents: in all 40, no entry of those days records the document's event. In about 5 of them an entry of those days is on the same matter (an embassy's telegram the day of the attack it reports; a memorandum on Vietnam planning the day of a planning order). In 26 of the 40, the calendar has no entry at all in the volume's thread on those days.

### What this means for the reading

- Group (b) is sound. These documents record events the calendar does not hold.
- Group (a) is weak. Only one match in ten is the document's own entry. Most matches show a document that sits beside an entry, on the same day or the same matter, not the record of that entry. A match by thread alone is right about one time in twenty-five.
- R8 makes each paper an entry of its own. So a reader should treat a document of group (a) like one of group (b): read it, and give it an entry unless the matched entry records the same event. The matched entries are where to look first, and where to cite the document if it is the record of one.
- Group (c) catches few transmittals. Most covering notes cannot be told from their headings, so readers will meet them as they read.
