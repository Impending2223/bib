# Numbered weeks: an American authority (research memo, Nov. 5, 2026)

The question: a year-week for each year of the calendar (YYYY-Www, as ISO 8601), with an American pedigree: the computation, the count, a prescriptive text, in force in or before 1960–69. Every finding below was read in the source named, by page or link; what is from memory is marked so. Downloads read from the scratchpad and deleted.

## Short answer

The public-health reporting week (today's "MMWR week") is the one. Weeks Sunday to Saturday; week 1 the week ending on the first Saturday that falls on Jan. 4–10, which is the same as "the first week with at least four days in the new year"; 52 weeks, or 53 in about one year in six. The Public Health Service numbered its weeks so from at least 1937 (the *Public Health Reports*) through the whole span (the *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report*, 1952–). The rule was written out, with its table of years 1948–80, in the MMWR of Jan. 6, 1965: "The 53rd Week", 13 MMWR 472. That note describes the practice and lays down the table; it cites no earlier text. The earliest prescriptive statement found in the CDC's own words, "MMWR week #1 of an MMWR year is the first week of the year that has at least four days in the calendar year", is the NNDSS fact sheet (by 2006). No federal standard (FIPS 4, 1968) or American National Standard (X3.30) of the span defines a week of the year. ISO's Monday-start rule (ISO/R 2015, 1971) was approved by the U.S. member body, after the span. Sunday as "the first day of the week" is the wording of the Sunday statutes McGowan v. Maryland reviews.

## 1. The MMWR week

### The rule as CDC states it now

- CDC, NNDSS, "MMWR Weeks" (fact sheet, 2 pp.), [CDC Stacks 22305](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/22305) (the PDF made Dec. 28, 2011; the same text at [ndc.services.cdc.gov](https://ndc.services.cdc.gov/wp-content/uploads/MMWR_week_overview.pdf)), p. 2, "Business rules for assigning MMWR week": "The first day of any MMWR week is Sunday"; numbering "to a maximum of 52 or 53"; "MMWR week #1 of an MMWR year is the first week of the year that has at least four days in the calendar year"; if Jan. 1 is Thursday, Friday or Saturday, its week "would be the last MMWR week of the previous year (#52 or #53)". P. 1: "the week of the epidemiologic year"; used for "the MMWR weekly morbidity tables and the annual Summary of Notifiable Diseases". No history, no date of adoption, no authority cited.
- The same text, word for word, in the Texas Department of State Health Services' copy, [MMWR_Week_Fact_Sheet.doc](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/disease/influenza/surveillance/2007-2008/MMWR_Week_Fact_Sheet.doc), filed under 2007–2008; the file's properties say last printed Nov. 14, 2006. So the fact sheet's text stood by 2006.
- New Mexico IBIS, [MMWR Week Description](https://ibis.doh.nm.gov/resource/MMWRWeekCalendar.html): the same rule, citing the CDC fact sheet as downloaded Mar. 7, 2016. The Tennessee fact sheet of 2012 (tn.gov) refused this environment (403): not read.

### The rule as the MMWR stated it in 1965

- *MMWR*, vol. 13, no. 53 (for release Jan. 6, 1965), "The 53rd Week", p. 472 ([Stacks 609](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/609)). The week ended Jan. 2, 1965 is "designated week Number 53 of 1964, and the first reporting week of 1965 is advanced to the week ending January 9." The reasoning: the Saturday "ending the reporting week advances one day in each common year and two days during a leap year"; in 1961 the first reporting week ended Jan. 7, 1962 Jan. 6, 1963 Jan. 5, 1964 Jan. 4. A table, "Date of the first Saturday falling on the 4th through the 10th day of each year", 1948–1980, the 28-year cycle 1948–75 and the start of the next; "Years of calendar adjustment, spanning 53 weeks, are underlined" (the underlining lost in the scan's text). The table's title is the rule: week 1 ends on the Saturday falling Jan. 4–10. Issued by the Communicable Disease Center, Atlanta (masthead). This is the nearest thing to a prescriptive text inside the span found so far.
- The same issue, p. 1 and Table 3: "53rd Week Ended January 2, 1965", cumulative "First 53 weeks, 1964", against "December 28, 1963" for 1963.

The diagnostic years: when Jan. 1 is Thursday to Saturday (1953, 1955, 1959, 1960, 1965, 1966) its week is the old year's last, not week 1; when it is Monday to Wednesday (1964) the week holding Dec. 29–31 is week 1 of the new year. That is the four-day rule, not "the week containing Jan. 1" and not "the first full week". Every first issue read fits the 1965 table. The week counts: 1952 had 53, by arithmetic (its 50th week ended Dec. 13, 1952: MMWR 1:50, [Stacks 426](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/426), record; 1953's week 1 then ends Jan. 10); 1964 had 53 (13:53).

Computed by the rule (not read), week 1 of each year of the span: 1960 Jan. 3–9; 1961 Jan. 1–7; 1962 Dec. 31, 1961–Jan. 6; 1963 Dec. 30, 1962–Jan. 5; 1964 Dec. 29, 1963–Jan. 4; 1965 Jan. 3–9; 1966 Jan. 2–8; 1967 Jan. 1–7; 1968 Dec. 31, 1967–Jan. 6; 1969 Dec. 29, 1968–Jan. 4. 53-week years in or near the span: 1958, 1964, 1969 (the table's Jan. 4 row). Against ISO 8601 (Monday weeks, same four-day rule): the week-years differ in count in 1959, 1969 and 1970 (ISO gives 1959 and 1970 53 weeks, 1969 52).

How the weeks were defined in the tables: "the total number of cases ... reported during the week usually ended the preceding Saturday" (MMWR 9:1, p. 8, "Source and Nature of Morbidity Data"; the same words in MMWR 1:1, p. 2). Deaths "by week of filing certificate" (9:1, tables 3–4, pp. 6–7). The annual summary charts the weeks by number with their ending dates ("WEEK NO." 4 ... 53 for 1964, "WK ENDED" 25 Jan. ...): MMWR 15:53, Annual Supplement: Summary 1966 (Nov. 1967), p. 46 ([Stacks 615](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/615)). Its "Sources of Data" defines no week.

### Before the MMWR: the Public Health Reports

- *Public Health Reports* 53 (Jan. 14, 1938) ([Stacks 69570](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/69570)), "Prevalence of Disease", cases telegraphed by State health officers "for weeks ended Jan. 1, 1938, and Jan. 2, 1937", the cumulative row "52 weeks" (p. 60). The week ended Saturday, Jan. 1, 1938 is the 52nd of 1937: 1937's week 1 ended Jan. 9, 1937, Jan. 1–2 (Friday, Saturday) left in 1936. The four-day rule in practice by 1937.
- PHR 53 (Jan. 21, 1938) ([Stacks 69603](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/69603)), "Deaths during week ended Jan. 1, 1938", "Total deaths, 52 weeks" (p. 98); the State table "for weeks ended Jan. 8, 1938, and Jan. 9, 1937" (from p. 102; the continuation page reads 103).
- PHR 64 (Dec. 30, 1949) ([Stacks 70776](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/70776)): "Reports from States for week ended December 10, 1949", "The cumulative total for 49 weeks of 1949" (p. 1671); deaths, "first 49 weeks of year" (p. 1675). So 1949's week 1 ended Jan. 8, 1949; Saturday, Jan. 1 left in 1948. The rule again.
- The publication's line: morbidity tables in PHR to Jan. 20, 1950, then the National Office of Vital Statistics' *Weekly Morbidity Report*, renamed the *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* in 1952; HEW approved the move to CDC Sept. 30, 1960 (Shaw et al., "A History of MMWR", [MMWR 2011;60(Suppl.)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/su6004a3.htm), read online, no page numbers taken). MMWR 1:1's running head is still "Weekly Morbidity Report" (p. 2). The 1960 issues are NOVS's (9:1, masthead); the 1964 ones CDC's, Atlanta (13:1, masthead).
- Who first fixed the rule, and in what text: not found. The PHR tables of 1937–38 number weeks by the rule without stating it. Recalled, not read: the Census Bureau's weekly city mortality reports (the *Weekly Health Index* of the 1930s) ran on the same Saturday weeks; whether the rule came from there is unknown.

### WHO, the League, CSTE

Not reached. A search found no WHO, League of Nations or PAHO text dating or defining the epidemiological week; the secondary pages found (software documentation) say only that PAHO and many Latin American countries use the Sunday week with the four-day rule and that WHO's practice varies. No CSTE resolution on the week was found.

## 2. FIPS PUB 4 (1968) and ANSI X3.30

- FIPS PUB 4, *Calendar Date*, Nov. 1, 1968. The 1968 scan was not found (NIST's `fipspub4.pdf` does not exist; archive.org holds FIPS 0, 1, 2 and 7 of the period but not 4). Read instead: the transcription EverySpec serves, [FIPS_PUB_4_3450](https://everyspec.com/NIST/NIST-FIPS/FIPS_PUB_4_3450/) (a Word file made Mar. 4, 1999, its properties naming NIST). The announcement: issued "under the direction of the Bureau of the Budget" under Pub. L. 89-306 and Circular A-86; approving authority the Bureau of the Budget; "prescribed for the interchange of formatted machine sensible coded data between and among agencies"; effective Jan. 1, 1970, earlier "by mutual agreement". The specifications: § 4, "a numeric code of six consecutive positions" (year, month, day: "640131"); § 5.2, month and day may be expressed "by a three digit Ordinal Number (beginning with 001, representing January 1 ...)"; § 5.3, year and ordinal day together. No week of the year; no day of the week. An ordinal day implies no week numbering.
- Its listing: FIPS PUB 0, *General Description of the Federal Information Processing Standards Register* (Nov. 1, 1968), the list of publications, last page (PDF p. 12): "FIPS PUB 4. Calendar Date, (Federal General Data Standard, Representations and codes). 20 cents" ([nvlpubs](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/FIPS/fipspub0.pdf)).
- FIPS PUB 4-1 (Jan. 27, 1988), "Supersedes FIPS PUB 4, 1968 November 1" (cover), adopting ANSI X3.30-1985, "Revision of ANSI X3.30-1971", reproduced in it ([nvlpubs](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/FIPS/fipspub4-1.pdf)): calendar date and ordinal date only; § 2.6, day of year "001 (January 1) through 365 or 366"; the cross index names ISO 2014, 2711, 3307, 4031, not ISO 2015 (numbering of weeks). No week. NIST's [withdrawn FIPS index](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2016/12/15/withdrawn_fips_by_numerical_order_index.pdf) lists "4 Calendar Date (ANSI X3.30-1968)", a designation not otherwise seen.
- ANSI X3.30-1971 itself: not read (no open copy found). That X3.30-1985 has no week makes it unlikely the 1971 text had one; not verified.

## 3. Before ISO, and the American practices

- ISO 2015:1976, *Numbering of weeks* (iTeh's free sample, the whole 2-page text, [ISO-2015-1976.pdf](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/6782/671ca05048bb4621bab8e1679c38424c/ISO-2015-1976.pdf)): replaces ISO Recommendation R 2015-1971, "technically identical"; R 2015 approved by member bodies including the "U.S.A." (Foreword, p. [ii]); Japan, Norway and Portugal disapproved. § 3.2: "the first day of a week shall be Monday"; § 3.3: "Week number one of a year is the first week containing four days or more of the new year" (p. 1); written "W01" etc. (§ 3.4). The Introduction gives its use: "Delivery dates in purchasing contracts, transport plans".
- ISO 8601:1988 (iTeh sample, [ISO-8601-1988.pdf](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/15903/9177845294f5474382a6461120cd10a6/ISO-8601-1988.pdf)): cancels ISO 2014, 2015, 2711, 3307 and 4031 (Foreword, p. ii); § 3.17, the calendar week starts Monday, the first is "the one that includes the first Thursday", "equivalent to the week which includes 4 January" (p. 2); § 5.2.3, the YYWwwD forms (p. 5).
- So the four-day rule is the same in ISO and the MMWR; only the first day differs (Monday, Sunday). An American authority within the span for the Sunday form is the PHS's; for the Monday form, none before the U.S. vote for R 2015 (1971).
- DIN 1355: not read. A 1999 Unicode list post (Otto Stolz, ["Re: First day of the week"](https://unicode.org/mail-arch/unicode-ml/Archives-Old/UML017/0107.html)) says the 1975 edition numbers the weekdays Monday 1 to Sunday 7 and bases the week number on Monday, and that the author found no copy of the 1943 edition. Swedish, Danish, Dutch practice: not reached.
- Retail 4-5-4: the National Retail Federation's page, [4-5-4 Calendar](https://nrf.com/resources/4-5-4-calendar): "derived in the 1930s during an informal inter-industry discussion"; "Many stores began using the 4-5-4 Calendar in the 1940s"; a 53rd week "if ... there are four or more days left in January during the 53rd week". A fiscal year of weeks ending in late January or early February, with a four-day test at its end; not a numbered week of the calendar year. The NRF's statement today, not a text of the period; the NRMA's own publication not found.
- The broadcast calendar: the Television Bureau of Advertising's calendar, [TVB_Bdcst_Calendar_2023-25.pdf](https://www.tvb.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/TVB_Bdcst_Calendar_2023-25.pdf), p. 1: "created in the 1960s"; "the standard week starts on Monday and ends on Sunday"; the billing month "always ends on the last Sunday of the calendar month". Months of weeks, not numbered weeks; no text of the 1960s found.
- Railroad car loadings: the AAR's weekly traffic reports today, as AJOT prints one with an editor's note ([week ending Jan. 3, 2026](https://www.ajot.com/news/aar-reports-rail-traffic-for-the-week-ending-january-03-2026)): "a week that bridges two different years is assigned to the year in which most of the days of that week fall"; the week ending Jan. 3, 2026 "was week 53 of 2025"; "annual totals are always weeks 1-52". Saturday-ending weeks, the majority (four-day) rule: the MMWR week in all but name. Whether the AAR numbered weeks so in the 1960s: not read.
- BLS, Census, the Federal Reserve's weekly series, the Weather Bureau's *Weekly Weather and Crop Bulletin*: no numbered week of the year found in any text read. Recalled, not read: the CPS reference week is "the week including the 12th" of the month and the Fed's weekly statements end on Wednesdays; neither numbers weeks of the year.

## 4. Sunday, "the first day of the week"

McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), read in the U.S. Reports ([LOC](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep366/usrep366420/usrep366420.pdf)):

- Warren, for the Court: New York's law of 1788 "omitted the term 'Lord's day' and substituted 'the first day of the week commonly called Sunday'" (2 Laws of N.Y. 1785–1788, 680), with similar changes in Maryland's (p. 434). Maryland's Code, art. 27: § 492 "Lord's day"; § 522 "Sabbath day" (p. 445); the 1696 act "for Sanctifying & keeping holy the Lord's Day Commonly called Sunday" (p. 446). The appendix prints the sections: § 492, "the Lord's day, commonly called Sunday" (p. 453); § 509 (Anne Arundel County), "on the first day of the week, commonly called Sunday" (p. 454); § 522, "on the Sabbath day, commonly called Sunday" (p. 456).
- Frankfurter, concurring: Pennsylvania's Great Law of 1682, "Every first day of the week, called the Lord's day" (p. 487); New York, 1788, again (p. 491). Appendix I, the colonial and early statutes by title: Pennsylvania's 1705 "Act to Restrain People from Labor on the First Day of the Week" (p. 548); Rhode Island's 1679 and 1798 "Act[s] Prohibiting Sports and Labour on the First Day of the Week" (pp. 548–549). Appendix II (from p. 551) tabulates the Sunday statutes then in force, but leaves out "definition provisions" (p. 551): it does not give their wording for the day.
- Douglas, dissenting, quoting Ex parte Newman, 9 Cal. 502 (1858): "the Christian voluntarily keeps holy the first day of the week" (p. 572).
- The companions (Two Guys v. McGinley, Braunfeld v. Brown, Gallagher v. Crown Kosher) not read.

- Whether the PHR weeks before 1937 (the series ran weekly tables from the 1910s, recalled) followed the rule; the Census Bureau's weekly mortality reports' rule.
- FIPS PUB 4 as printed in 1968 (only a 1999 transcription read); ANSI X3.30-1971's text.
- The AAR's, the NRMA's and the broadcasters' texts of the 1960s; DIN 1355 (1943, 1975); WHO's and the League's weeks.
