# The press's hours, 1960-68: when a paper first reached readers in Washington, and where the calendar should place it

Written Nov. 11-12, 2026, for the owner's questions of Nov. 11, 2026 (evening, later): "when was the NYT first distributed in D.C.? ... Does that push us over the midnight line?" and the release days of the *National Guardian* and the *Amsterdam News*. Open sources only (archive.org full text and its full-text search, the Library of Congress's loc.gov API). HathiTrust and the old Chronicling America pages sit behind a bot check and were not used; the Google Books API refused with "too many requests". Where a book is lending-only, the quotation is from archive.org's full-text search (the link carries the search; no page number is shown).

**Does the Times cross midnight?** In New York, yes: the first edition was on sale in Manhattan the evening before its date. In Washington, no: nothing found puts physical copies of the Times on sale in the District before midnight, and the Washington Post itself learned what the Times was printing that night only by a wirephoto of its front page. On the D.C. rule the Times stays on the morning of its date.

## The New York Times

**In New York, the first edition, the evening before.**
- To press 9:30 p.m. weekdays, Sundays 7 p.m. Saturday (1958): "The early edition of the New York Times which we receive in Vermont goes to press at 9:30 p.m. weekdays, the Sunday edition at 7 Saturday night." Letter, *Middlebury Campus*, Mar. 6, 1958 (https://archive.org/details/middleburyNewspapers_1958-03-06). A reader's letter, not the Times; it agrees with what follows.
- On the street about 9:45 p.m. (Szulc's Cuba story, set against a CBS broadcast by Novins; apparently Apr. 1961): "Szulc would appear in the first edition of The New York Times, hitting the street at about 9:45 p.m." Harrison E. Salisbury, *Without Fear or Favor* (1980) (https://archive.org/details/withoutfearorfav00sali?q=%22hitting+the+street%22).
- On sale at 11 p.m. (1969): "11:00 p.m. The first edition of the New York Times goes on sale on the streets of the city." Zimmerman and Schaap, *The Year the Mets Lost Last Place* (1969) (https://archive.org/details/yearmetslostlast0000zimm?q=%22goes+on+sale+on+the+streets%22).
- At the Daily News by 10 p.m. (1973, after the period): "at 10 p.m. when the first edition of the Times arrived". *[More]*, Aug. 1973 (https://archive.org/details/sim_more_1973-08_3_8).
- The publisher read it at home the same night: "When Punch Sulzberger received his Times early edition at home that night" (Talese, *The Kingdom and the Power* (1969), https://archive.org/details/kingdompower00tale?q=%22early+edition%22); he read Herbert Matthews's editorial on the Christmas truce of Dec. 1966 "in the early edition of the Times, he called John at home and ordered him to kill it" (Tifft and Jones, *The Trust* (1999), https://archive.org/details/trustprivatea00tiff?q=%22early+edition%22). An editorial could thus be in the early edition and gone from the Late City Edition, the edition on microfilm and in TimesMachine.

**In Washington, the next morning.**
- 1946, the Times's own advertisement in the Post: "A new, fast, air service has just been inaugurated which brings the Late City Edition of The New York Times to Washington readers before breakfast ... flown to Washington daily (weather permitting) ... arrives in Washington less than two hours after it leaves the presses ... available at all leading news dealers in the nation's capital." *Washington Post*, Mar. 11, 1946 (https://archive.org/details/per_washington-post_1946-03-11_25470). The edition sent was the Late City, not the first edition.
- 1960s-70s: the Post knew what the Times would print only by wire: "The way THE POST knew what the Times was printing that evening was that each night it received from New York via UPI wirephoto a picture of page 1 of the Times' first edition, on the street early enough for THE POST to catch up in its main edition. The Times received a similar facsimile from Washington." Chalmers M. Roberts, *In the Shadow of Power* (1989), in the Bradlee years (the passage turns on his having "no opinion" on the war) (https://archive.org/details/inshadowofpowers0000robe?q=%22UPI+wirephoto%22). "On the street" is the New York street. Had copies been on sale in the District that evening, the wirephoto would not have been needed.
- Kennedy's papers came at waking: he "got up between seven-thirty and eight in the morning. He was immediately handed the morning papers" (Salinger, *With Kennedy* (1966), https://archive.org/details/withkennedy0000pier?q=%22seven-thirty+and+eight+in+the+morning%22). Officials often knew the next day's Times the night before (by wirephoto, from the Times bureau, from calls), but that is not public distribution.
- Not found: a 1960s statement of the hour the Times reached D.C. newsstands, or of any D.C. sale of the first edition the evening before. Editor & Publisher, the Times histories (Talese; Tifft and Jones; Salisbury) and Reedy, Salinger and Kearns were searched for it. A Newsweek line of 1967, "when the Times arrives in Washington at about noon", is about the *Los Angeles* Times (it goes on to the LA Times-Washington Post news service of 1962), not the New York Times.
- The Western Edition (Oct. 1, 1962-Jan. 1964) was printed in Los Angeles, on the day of its date: "even a fractional Times on the day it was printed" (*Columbia Journalism Review* 1, no. 4 (Winter 1963), "New York Times out west", https://archive.org/details/sim_columbia-journalism-review_winter-1962_1_4). It does not touch Washington.

**Recommendation.** Place a Times story at the early morning of its date (the D.C. rule). Where the story itself became public earlier in New York and that matters, a note can say "First edition on sale in New York the evening before". A story found only in the first edition (killed later) is an evening-before item in New York, with no D.C. distribution. Confidence: moderate. The morning hour in D.C. rests on the 1946 advertisement and on the Post's nightly wirephoto. No 1960s timetable was found.

## The Washington Post

- "the bulldog edition of the Post, which hits the street at 9:30 p.m. Minutes later Bundy called the Post to complain." Ben H. Bagdikian, "Press Independence and the Cuban Crisis," *Columbia Journalism Review* 1, no. 4 (Winter 1963): 9 (the Khrushchev letter on the IL-28s, Nov. 1962) (https://archive.org/details/sim_columbia-journalism-review_winter-1962_1_4). The White House read it and called within minutes, so the bulldog was public and acted on the evening before its date.
- The hour from the 1954 merger: after an early paper and "a somewhat less chaotic 7 P.M. edition. This lasted nine months before publication time was moved back to 9:30 P.M. The point of all this strain was simple: to keep as much of the Times-Herald" circulation (Roberts, *In the Shadow of Power*, https://archive.org/details/inshadowofpowers0000robe?q=%22publication+time%22). The bulldog, "off the press each evening to catch the after-theater crowd", was the edition a President scanned "before retiring" (the same, ?q=%22after-theater+crowd%22; which President is not shown in the search snippet).
- Johnson: at the White House, in the evening, "We went into his bedroom, where on the turned-down bed lay the early edition of the Post, with a large headline saying that Walter Tobriner, the lead commissioner for the District ..." Katharine Graham, *Personal History* (1997) (https://archive.org/details/personalhistory0000grah?q=%22turned-down+bed%22). Kearns has Johnson working from the AP ticker to "insist upon a corrected story in the late city edition" (*Lyndon Johnson and the American Dream* (1976), https://archive.org/details/lyndonjohnsoname00good?q=%22corrected+story%22).

**Recommendation.** A Post story known to have run in the bulldog: the evening before its date, 9:30 p.m. A Post story whose edition is not known: early morning of its date, by the owner's default. The copy in the archives is likely a later edition, so its story may postdate the bulldog (not verified here). Confidence: high for the 9:30 hour, 1955-63; moderate for 1964-68 (the Graham anecdote, no hour).

## The Evening Star

- An afternoon paper "With Sunday Morning Edition" (its masthead, in the Library of Congress's film). The filmed weekday copy is a mid-afternoon edition: the front page of Oct. 23, 1962 reports "High, 66, at 2 p.m. today" (https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1962-10-23/ed-1/?sp=1&st=text).
- Not found: the hour of its first weekday edition.

**Recommendation.** Weekday Star: afternoon of its date; a story is safe at 3 p.m. or later, the hour of the filmed edition. Sunday Star: early morning of its date. Confidence: moderate.

## The National Guardian

- 1960-62: dated Monday, printed the Tuesday before. "The GUARDIAN goes to press each week on Tuesday; it is dated the following Monday, for example, this issue, dated Dec. 11, was printed Tues. morning, Dec. 5. To be certain that your copy arrives in time for Tuesday publication ..." *National Guardian*, Dec. 11, 1961, "Notice to West Coast Advertisers" (https://archive.org/details/sim_guardian_1961-12-11_14_9). The masthead: "Published weekly by Weekly Guardian Associates, Inc., 197 E. 4th St., New York 9". Every issue of 1960-61 on archive.org (103) is dated a Monday.
- The cover date then moved. Dates cited in texts on archive.org fall on Mondays through Aug. 6, 1962, on Thursdays from Dec. 6, 1962 through Nov. 7, 1963, and on Saturdays from Mar. 7, 1964 through 1969: a tally, not a run of the paper, which archive.org has only for 1948-61. Whether the press day stayed Tuesday is not known. Belfrage and Aronson, *Something to Guard* (1978), is lending-only, and its full text gave nothing on the point.

**Recommendation.** 1960-62: release day the Tuesday six days before the Monday cover date (printed that morning, then mailed; readers had it later in the week). 1963-68: provisionally the Tuesday before the cover date (two days before a Thursday date, four before a Saturday), with "Check: release day". An issue's masthead or notices of 1963-68 would settle it. Confidence: high for 1960-62, low for 1963-68. This is not early morning of the cover date: the Guardian's cover date was later than its release.

## The Amsterdam News

- Cover-dated Saturday throughout 1960-68: of the dates cited in texts on archive.org, nearly all fall on Saturdays each year (25-38 a year), e.g. "New York Amsterdam News, Saturday, Mar. 31, 1962, p. 15" (*The John Coltrane Reference*, https://archive.org/details/johncoltranerefe0000unse). In the repository: June 4, 1960 is a Saturday. The July 19, 1964 cite in `lists/cal/196407.yaml` (via Branch) is a Sunday, perhaps an extra; check it.
- Not found: the day it reached the stands or the mail. No masthead, Ayer or Editor & Publisher listing was open (Ayer's 1969 directory is lending-only). The common account that Saturday-dated Black weeklies were on the stands earlier in the week was not borne out by any source found. The only "came out on Thursday" hit was a novel.

**Recommendation.** Keep the Saturday cover date, early morning, with "Check: release day". Confidence: low. If the release day is found earlier in the week (as with the Guardian), move the paper's items to that day as a release-day band.

## Not found, and where to look next

The hour the Times reached D.C. newsstands in the 1960s; the Times's out-of-town circulation reports of the decade (Editor & Publisher items on its air and rail delivery); the Star's first-edition hour; the Guardian's press day after 1962; the Amsterdam News's release day. Places to look: Times Talk (the Times house organ), Ayer's or Editor & Publisher's Year Books of the 1960s (edition times and publication days), the Guardian's and the Amsterdam News's own mastheads on microfilm, and the Library of Congress's Evening Star film for 1964-72.
