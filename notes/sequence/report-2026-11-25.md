# The sequence pass: links per month (Nov. 25, 2026)

For the owner. You asked on Nov. 24 whether a pass could "put them together, read them, and ask yourself 'what goes after what?'" This is the count I promised, so that you can judge whether the thinner months are worth the cost. A paragraph or a list item is one line.

## What was done

- The rule is R25c in the reader's brief. A link (`follows:`) says that one entry of a day comes directly after another.
- For each month a Sonnet reader reads every day's entries together and writes the links. An Opus auditor then checks every link against the two entries and, where their words do not settle the order, against the sources. The audited patch is merged.
- 80 of the calendar's 136 months have been read and audited, the largest first. They hold 46,289 of the calendar's 58,881 entries.
- All 80 are merged into the tree. PR #219 put 23 months on the site at 3:20 a.m. and PR #220 the other 57 at 5:50 a.m.

## What it yielded

- The readers wrote 1,654 links. After the audits the patches hold 2,036. The audits also took 15 links out of the tree that were there before the pass and were wrong.
- The most came from the campaign months of 1960 and from the weeks of a crisis: Oct. 1960, 157, Sept. 1960, 140, Nov. 1960, 78, Nov. 1963, 76. The middle month gives 20 links.
- The thinnest months gave about one link in a hundred entries: May 1967, 4 in 493, Feb. 1968, 6 in 571, Aug. 1967, 6 in 530, Sept. 1964, 6 in 523, Aug. 1965, 7 in 587.
- The audits made 555 other changes on the way. Most are a sentence in a note that names the page a link rests on. The rest are hours set from a source, a "Check:" where two sources disagree, and a few corrections of fact. Three examples of the last: two Senate entries of May 24, 1967 gave the House's hour of adjournment; Zhou's entry of Nov. 30, 1966 named Chen Boda where the chronology names Chen Yi; Johnson's message to Wilson of June 14, 1966 says a visit in "mid- or late July", where the entry said late July.

## What the audits showed about the readers

- A reader's link taken from the entries' own words holds about nine times in ten.
- A reader's link taken from the order in which a chronology prints a day's items mostly does not hold. In January 1964 the audit struck 16 of 36. R25c now says that a printed order alone is no order.
- The readers open few sources. In the last three waves most opened none. The auditors open many: seven to ten books or volumes for a month is common. That is where their added links come from.
- A reader sometimes links only one country's entries and leaves the rest of the month unread for order. The launch note to the auditor now names the days a reader left without links.

## Defaults taken

All five are under ruling 55 in `notes/owner-rulings.md`. Each lists the links it covers, so that if you rule the other way they can be struck or put back by rule.

1. The order in which a collection prints a day's items (a volume of speeches, a chronology compiled later) is no order of the day by itself.
2. The serial numbers of one body's acts of a day are no order by themselves.
3. A diary is its writer's own account of the day. The order of its sentences counts where the hours on the pages read stand in that order and nothing goes against it. It is the weakest kind of basis, and the note says so.
4. The pages of the Congressional Record within one chamber's day are the order of the debate, unless they print a section apart.
5. A note-taker's record of a session (Malin's notes of the Presidium) shows the order only where the notes are in his own hand throughout. Where he wrote under agenda headings typed beforehand, the order is the agenda's and shows nothing.

## What it costs, and the months left

- A month costs one Sonnet read (about 230,000 tokens and ten minutes), one Opus audit (about 300,000 tokens and fifteen to twenty minutes) and three minutes of merging.
- The 56 months not yet read are the smaller ones, each under 440 entries. At the rate of the thin months they would give 5 to 15 links each.
- The audits give more than links. Each one lists, for a later pass, the entries that hold two acts and need splitting, the hours a source gives that an entry lacks, the pairs that look like one event entered twice, and the days on which sources disagree. 80 audit notes hold such lists (`notes/sequence/audit-<month>.md`). In the thin months these lists are most of the value.
- The default I have taken (ruling 55) is to carry on with the remaining months after 5 p.m. today, the largest first. Tell me if you would rather stop at a size, or stop.

## What the months done leave for a later pass

- Entries to split (R25b). Most of the audit notes name some.
- Likely duplicates to compare and fold together. Most of the audit notes name some.
- Chinese entries that have a `time` and no `time_zone`. The stored hour is right and the site sorts by it, but the day sheet the readers work from prints it in New York time, and a part of the day drawn for such an entry can fall in the wrong clock (June 1960: eight Bucharest entries drawn in Peking's). This is a fields pass, not a reading pass.
- Events a source reports that have no entry. Several audits name some; they go to readers.
