## 56. The day's order: a reception after its transmission; a printed order the weakest of presumptions (the owner, Nov. 25, 2026)

The owner, Nov. 25, 2026, about 10:45 a.m. PT, marked "Immediate.": "The \"watching an event doesn't 'follow:' the event\" rule should be reversed, and the entries it affects re-ruled. It may be true as a matter of time/temporal contemporaneity alone, but as a matter of legibility, putting the reception after the transmission is better default rule than neutrality. I'm also not sure that a rule against a chronology or volume order makes sense. It should be only the weakest of presumptions, but if a volume is otherwise in chronological sequence, doesn't that suggest that the editors believe their sort is more likely than the alternative?"

**What it reverses.** Two things in R25c as it stood on the night of Nov. 24–25. First, the sentence that an act watched or heard as it happens is of the same hour and no order (written from the staff watching Truman's telecast, `cal.1960-07-02.elections-1960-nomination-13`). Second, ruling 55's first default, that the order in which a collection prints a day's items is no order of the day by itself.

**The rule now (R25c).** A reception comes after its transmission: the act of watching, hearing or reading an event as it happens follows the event's entry. The order in which a chronology or a volume prints a day's items is a presumption, the weakest one: where the volume is otherwise in the order of time, an item printed after another of the same day follows it unless something goes against it, and anything does (the items' words, an hour, a part of the day, the route, a diary, another source). A link on the printed order alone says "the printed order alone" in its note, with the page, so that the links can be found by those words.

**Readings taken, each a default the owner can overrule.**

1. An act done during an event, whose own line names the event ("during the Hoover–King meeting"), also follows the event's entry. The owner's ground is legibility over neutrality, and such a line cannot be read before the event it names. Where it applies: the link of Dec. 1, 1964 that the audit took out on the old sentence (`cal.1964-12-01.press-and-broadcasting` after `cal.1964-12-01.king`), and any like it the re-ruling finds. If the owner means reception only, those links are named in `notes/sequence/rerule/` under "during".
2. The second default of ruling 55 (the serial numbers of one body's acts of a day) and its fifth (the item order of an agenda typed before a session) are left as they were: no order by themselves. The owner's words name a chronology and a volume, whose editors sort by time; a serial number and an agenda are not an editor's sort. If the owner's ruling reaches them, the links struck on them are listed under those two defaults and go back by rule.
3. A volume that is shown not to keep the order of the day carries no presumption on the days in hand (*Freedom of Communications*, Part 1, on Oct. 3, 12 and 22, 1960, where the texts show another order).
4. The printed order is taken from the volume itself, on the page, not from the order in which an earlier reader cited or entered the items.
5. A narrative book is not a chronology or a collection sorted by time. The order of its pages counts for a day only where the book tells that day through in order, and the basis then names the book as a narrative.
